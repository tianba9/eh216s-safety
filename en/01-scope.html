Define Responsibility, Then Mission

The most common mistake in safety analysis is to begin by “adding redundancy”. The correct starting point is two things: allocate safety responsibility clearly among the organisations, and define “what is flown and where” in enough detail to be checked item by item. The former determines the management system; the latter determines the boundary of the hazard list.

01Safety Culture & Responsibility Allocation

With no pilot on board, the traditional “pilot–aircraft” chain of responsibility is broken. Responsibility must be reassigned, otherwise a vacuum arises in which “everyone is responsible and no one is”.

This table reveals a key structure: the four certificates are not four independent licences, but an institutional device that allocates safety responsibility across four parties. TC locks down design, PC locks down manufacturing, AC locks down the state of each individual aircraft, and OC locks down operational capability. If any one certificate is missing, one link in the chain of responsibility is broken.

Three observable indicators of safety culture

  • Reporting culture: whether a non-punitive occurrence-reporting channel exists. Without systematic feedback, the operating data from 90,000 sorties are merely flight records, not a safety asset.
  • Just culture: distinguishing “mistakes” from “violations”. A judgement error by the remote crew during emergency takeover should feed training improvement rather than blame.
  • Learning culture: whether design changes after certification still undergo full hazard reassessment. Design changes during the continuing-airworthiness phase are the most easily overlooked entry point for risk.
A structural problem

With no pilot on board, the passengers are the only people on the aircraft, yet have no means of handling any situation. This means the cabin design must assume “occupants are entirely passive”: procedures such as “please assist the crew in opening the door” cannot be used as a backstop. This is the fundamental difference from piloted aircraft in passive-safety design.

Functions are the object of hazard analysis. If functions are not described clearly, hazards cannot be enumerated. The table below breaks them down by the four groups — “flight, energy, control and support” — to an analysable level of granularity.

F3 and F4 are safety-critical functions, corresponding directly to catastrophic hazards; F6 and F7 are hazardous; the remainder are major or minor. This preliminary judgement will be quantified with FHA in Chapter 03.

03Operating Design Conditions (ODC)

ODC is the input describing “under what conditions the aircraft is designed”, and is the mirror image of the ODD (the conditions permitted in operation). The clearer the design boundary, the more enforceable the operational limits.

Table 1-3　Operating design conditions and their safety implications
Condition classDesign conditionSafety implication
AirspaceLow-altitude segregated airspace, time- and zone-shared with piloted aircraftReduces the catastrophic hazard of “mid-air collision” to a manageable level
AltitudeLow-altitude operation, with a specific ceiling subject to authority approvalLimits fall energy and the ground footprint
WeatherVisual meteorological conditions, with limits on wind speed and visibilityAvoids disturbances beyond the control capability
VertiportHard, level ground, with clearance and crowd-separation distancesControls third-party risk on the ground
Time of dayDaylight operationA scenario constraint relying on vision and visual sensing
CommunicationsWithin the coverage of the dedicated linkEnsures remote monitoring and takeover remain available
ElectromagneticNon-severely disturbed environmentPrevents interference with the link and navigation

Note: no specific figures (such as an upper wind-speed limit or crowd-separation distance) are given in the table. No quantitative figures appear in public material, and this report does not speculate; they are listed merely as parameters to be verified later with the authority or the operator.

04Scenario Library

Scenarios are not marketing categories but ways in which hazards combine. The same aircraft, in different scenarios, is exposed to different sets of hazards.

Table 1-4　Six typical operating scenarios and their dominant hazards
No.ScenarioEnvironmental characteristicsDominant hazards
S1Scenic circuit sightseeingFixed circuit, sparse crowd below, single take-off and landing pointGround personnel entering the rotor area during take-off and landing; low-altitude wind disturbance
S2Point-to-point short-haul shuttleBetween two banks or two districts, crossing water or roadsPower loss en route; difficulty selecting a diversion area
S3Urban landmark sightseeingDense buildings, complex downwash, complex electromagnetic environmentCollision with obstacles; link interference
S4Emergency medical transferTime pressure, non-standard take-off and landing pointsLanding at non-standard sites; procedural shortcuts due to haste
S5Training and test flightsEdge of the envelope, deliberate fault injectionDeliberately created out-of-limit states
S6Multi-aircraft operations at one siteSimultaneous take-offs and landings at several points in one scenic areaAirspace conflicts; ground-scheduling errors

S3 and S6 are the two highest-risk scenarios: the former exposes the aircraft to complex building wakes and electromagnetic conditions, while the latter upgrades “single-aircraft safety” to “system safety”, requiring ground scheduling to take on the conflict-prevention role.

The matrix is read by rows and columns: the rows for F3, F4 and F9 are filled throughout, indicating that they are common critical functions across all scenarios and should be assigned the highest design-assurance level first; the S3 column has the most filled cells, indicating that the urban scenario is the most hazard-dense operating environment and the focus of ODD definition.

06Chapter Summary

Safety responsibility is redistributed among four parties: the designer owns design assurance, the manufacturer owns production consistency, the operator owns operations, and the regulator owns certification and oversight. With no pilot on board, passengers are entirely passive — a constraint that runs through all subsequent design.

The function inventory is broken down to F1–F10, the ODC set out the design boundary, and the scenario library gives six combinations. The matrix shows that F3 (attitude), F4 (energy) and F9 (occupant protection) are critical in every scenario, and that the S3 urban scenario is the most hazard-dense.
