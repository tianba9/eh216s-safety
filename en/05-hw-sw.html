Hardware by Isolation, Software by Constraint

The general solution for hardware safety is “detect – isolate – degrade”, because hardware failures are random and statistical. Software failures are not random: once present, they necessarily recur and will manifest in all three systems at once. Therefore the solution for software safety is not redundancy but constraints on the development process.

01Hardware Failure Modes & Protection

The table below lists the main failure modes, detection means and protective actions by subsystem. It is a condensed FMEA and also an input to the maintenance programme.

Table 5-1　Hardware failure modes, detection and protection
ComponentMain failure modeDetection meansProtective action
MotorWinding short circuit, bearing wear, demagnetisationPhase current, speed, temperature and vibration monitoringDerated operation or isolation, control reconfiguration
ESCPower transistor breakdown, drive failureBus current anomaly, MOS temperatureShut down that channel, transfer load to the opposite side
Rotor / bladeCrack, fatigue fracture, foreign-object strikeGround inspection + vibration-spectrum anomalyCannot be repaired in flight; degrade according to remaining thrust
Structure / armComposite delamination, loose fasteners, fatigue crackPeriodic non-destructive testing + strain monitoringLimit the envelope, shorten the inspection interval
BatteryInternal short circuit, capacity fade, thermal runawayCell voltage, temperature, internal resistance, gas/pressureIsolate that group, disconnect the charge/discharge circuit
Power distributionContactor welding, wiring chafe, insulation ageingCircuit current, insulation-resistance monitoringStaged power-down, retain critical loads
SensorDrift, seizure, common-mode errorMulti-source cross-validation, plausibility checksReject the abnormal source, switch the fusion strategy

One pattern is worth noting: apart from the blades, almost all hardware failures can be detected and isolated in flight. Blades are the exception — once one breaks, no software means can remedy it; only ground inspection and life management apply. That is why the inspection intervals and discard criteria for the rotor system are far stricter in passenger-carrying operations than in industrial drones.

02Single-Point & Latent Faults

When assessing a hardware safety architecture, two metrics are more meaningful than “how many backups there are”: the Single-Point Fault Metric (SPFM) and the Latent Fault Metric (LFM). They originated in automotive functional safety, but their logic applies fully to aviation.

Table 5-2　Meaning of the two fault metrics and their application to this aircraft
MetricQuestion answeredKey point for this aircraftDirection of improvement
SPFM
Single-Point Fault Metric		How many failures would directly cause a safety objective to be lostSingle-arm structure, single power-distribution group, single sensorRaise diagnostic coverage, eliminate single points
LFM
Latent Fault Metric		How many failures go undetected when they occur, until combined with another faultHidden faults in voting channels, standby channel not activatedPower-on self-test + periodic self-test + cross-monitoring

Latent faults are more insidious and more dangerous than single-point faults. For example, if the second flight-control channel remains in a “standby but not self-tested” state for a long time, its fault will not be found; it is exposed only when the first channel fails and the second is needed — by which time there is no way back. This is why “triple redundancy” looks elegant on paper but is difficult in engineering: the effectiveness of redundancy depends on every backup being continuously verified.

03Software Safety Design

The flight-control software is developed to Level A, meaning that requirements traceability, coverage and independent verification must all be satisfied. But process compliance does not equal correct logic; the software-level safety design is broken down by function below.

Table 5-3　Software functions, failure consequences and design constraints
Software functionFailure consequenceDesign constraintVerification method
Control lawAttitude divergence, oscillationEnvelope protection, command limiting, anti-windupHardware-in-the-loop + flight-test envelope expansion
Voting logicWrong channel connectedFault detectability as a prerequisite, alarm on any disagreementFault-injection tests
Navigation fusionPosition drift, entry into the wrong airspaceMulti-source consistency checks, degradation strategyGNSS jamming/spoofing injection tests
Mission managementWrong route, boundary violationWaypoint validity checks, geofencingScenario replay and boundary test cases
Health managementMissed or false fault reportsThreshold hysteresis, multi-parameter joint criteriaFault injection + operating-data replay
Fail-safeShould divert but does notConservative criteria, trigger on failureFull-scenario drills

Software common causes: the hardest class of risk to defend against

Random hardware failures can be handled by multiplying probabilities; software cannot. A defect in a piece of code appears with 100% probability on every execution — if three systems run the same software, their “independence” equals zero at that moment.

Three resulting engineering requirements

Heterogeneous development: different teams, languages or algorithms implement the same function, so that the same defect is unlikely to exist in two places; independent verification: the verifier must not be the developer and should not share the same test cases; diverse voting: compare not only output values but also intermediate quantities (such as attitude angular rate and control commands), so that deviations surface earlier.

04Perception & SOTIF

Safety of the Intended Functionality (SOTIF) deals with the risk that “the system has no fault, but its performance is insufficient for the scenario”. For low-altitude aircraft, such risks concentrate in perception and decision-making.

Table 5-4　Typical SOTIF scenarios and mitigation
ScenarioWhy it poses a riskMitigation
Thin wires, cablesExtremely small visual and radar cross-section, hard to detect at rangePre-survey the route, avoid known cable corridors
Backlighting and strong reflectionsVisual sensing saturated, reflections from water or glass curtain wallsMulti-sensor fusion, time-of-day restrictions
Rain, fog and low visibilityReduced sensing range and reduced aerodynamic efficiency at the same timeWeather limits + visual meteorological conditions only
Other low-altitude aircraftNon-cooperative targets without transpondersSegregated airspace + unified ground scheduling
Bird flocksSudden, unpredictable, high relative speedVertiport ecological management + sensing alerts
Building wakes and downwashLocal disturbances beyond the control marginRoute avoidance + wind-disturbance estimation

None of the items in this table can be solved by “adding redundancy”. Their common solution is narrowing the operating envelope — excluding uncontrollable scenarios from the ODD. That is why the safety strategy for flying vehicles is essentially “trading operating restrictions for design simplification”.

05Remote-Crew Human Factors

With no pilot on board, the human's role changes from “operator” to “monitor and decision-maker”. This shift brings a new class of risk: vigilance decay in monitoring tasks.

Table 5-5　Three classes of human-factor risk for the remote crew
RiskMechanismMitigation
Loss of situation awarenessLong periods of normal flight reduce attention, slowing reaction to an anomalyShift rotation, alarm grading, regular scenario drills
Takeover delayA delay of seconds to tens of seconds between detecting an anomaly and forming a responsePreset response plans, one-touch operations, graded authority
Multi-aircraft workloadAttention divided when one person monitors several aircraftLimit the aircraft-to-operator ratio, automatic alarm prioritisation

The special condition lists the “remote-crew interface” as one of the seven requirement categories and requires the ground control station to provide real-time monitoring and emergency takeover functions Standard. This shows that the authority has recognised: on this class of aircraft, the human is not a backup but part of the system, whose reliability must be designed, verified and bounded.

06Chapter Summary

The general solution for hardware safety is detect–isolate–degrade, because hardware failures are random and statistical. Software is different: once a defect exists it necessarily recurs and manifests in all three systems at once — so software safety relies on development constraints, not redundancy.

SPFM and LFM are more meaningful than “how many backups there are”, and latent faults often surface only when a backup is needed. None of the items in the SOTIF table can be solved by adding redundancy; the solution is always to narrow the operating envelope.
