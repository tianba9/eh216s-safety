Redundancy Is Designing What Happens After Failure

The redundancy of the EH216-S appears at three levels: three flight-control channels, 12 battery groups and three data links. But the value of redundancy does not depend on the count; it depends on whether the system can remain controllable after a failure — this chapter turns the “fault-tolerance boundary” into a concrete number.

01Architecture Overview

The table below groups the publicly disclosed redundancy measures by system and notes the corresponding safety objectives. Any inference at the level of functional requirements is explained in the remarks.

Table 4-1　Redundancy measures and the corresponding safety objectives

Overall character of the architecture

This is a redundancy scheme of “multiple independent layers, running vertically through the system”: triple redundancy at the control layer, multiple isolated groups at the energy layer, triple at the communications layer, autonomous protection at the decision layer, and manual takeover at the outermost layer. Each layer can independently handle one class of failure, but no single layer can handle them all — that is the meaning of layered redundancy.

02Choosing a Redundancy Scheme

A redundancy scheme is not chosen arbitrarily; it is determined by two conditions: “can the fault be detected” and “is the failure safe”. The table below lists common schemes and the conditions under which they apply.

Table 4-2　Comparison of common redundancy schemes
SchemeMeaningAdvantageLimitationApplicability to the EH216-S
1oo1Single channel; a failure is a failureSimple, lightNo fault toleranceNot used for safety-critical functions
1oo2Either of two channels acting takes effectHigh availabilityHigh spurious-operation rateNot applicable (spurious operation is fatal with people on board)
2oo2Both channels must act for the output to take effectHigh safetyLow availabilityCan serve as auxiliary voting
2oo3Two-out-of-three voting; tolerates one wrong channelBalances safety and availabilityDepends on faults being detectableThe reasonable choice for the main flight-control channel
1oo2DTwo channels with diagnostics; degrades on faultHigh diagnostic coverageDiagnostics themselves can failCan be used for secondary functions

The 2oo3 vote formed by three flight-control channels is the typical choice for a passenger-carrying application: it can tolerate one channel producing a wrong output without affecting the flight, while retaining two healthy channels to continue working. But it has an implicit premise — at most one of the three is wrong. If two are wrong at the same time and in the same direction, the vote will judge the wrong result to be correct. That is the killing point of common-cause failure.

03Voting & Degradation Paths

The voting mechanism must be paired with a degradation strategy. After a channel disagreement is detected, the system must answer three questions: whom to trust, whether to degrade, and to what state to degrade.

Table 4-3　Fault states and degradation strategies
Fault stateVoting resultDegradation actionRisk handling
All three channels agreeNormal outputNoneFly as planned
One channel disagreesTwo-out-of-two passes; disagreeing channel isolatedRetain two channels, reduce the envelopeContinue the mission or return early
Two channels disagree in the same directionThe vote may be wrongMust rely on the third channel's self-check and monitoringGreatest risk point, see the next section
Link interruption—Enter the preset fail-safe procedureHover and wait / autonomous diversion to the nearest site
Energy anomaly—Isolate the faulty group, reduce power demandShorten the route, land at the nearest site
Unrecoverable—Trigger the emergency landing procedureSelect the minimum-risk diversion area

The third row of the table is the most fragile cell of the whole architecture. It means: the safety of 2oo3 ultimately depends on “whether two same-direction errors can be identified”. This capability comes from the third channel's independent self-check, cross-validation of sensors, or the judgement of the ground remote crew. Of the three means, the first two are automatic and the third is constrained by link delay.

04Focus: Flight-Control Redundancy & Degradation

This section puts cut set A1 and the control structure of Chapter 03 into concrete numbers: when one rotor, one arm or even more propulsion units fail, can the aircraft still fly?

Thrust margin is the physical basis of fault tolerance

At a maximum take-off weight of 620 kg with 16 rotors, each rotor must provide about 380 N of thrust in hover. A margin must be designed in, otherwise wind disturbance, climb and failures cannot be handled. This report assumes a maximum available thrust of 500 N per rotor (about 30% margin) and uses this to estimate hover feasibility under different failure cases.

Table 4-4　Thrust margin under different failure cases (author's estimate)
Failure caseRemaining rotorsTotal available thrustMarginCan hover be maintained
Intact168000 N31.5%Yes
Single rotor failure157500 N23.3%Yes, with torque compensation
One arm lost (2 rotors)147000 N15.1%Yes, but attitude margin falls significantly
Two arms lost (4 rotors)126000 N-1.4%No, immediate landing required

Note: total thrust demand is 6,082 N (620 kg × 9.81). The table does not include the extra derating caused by torque compensation, so the actual usable margin is lower than the figures shown. The 500 N maximum rotor thrust is an assumption, not published data.

Three inferences from this

First, a single rotor failure is tolerable, consistent with the statement of “full backup of key components”; second, the real fault-tolerance boundary is near “the loss of two arms”, where the thrust margin turns from positive to negative and any attempt to continue flying will make height unsustainable; third, therefore the Fail-Safe criterion cannot look only at “whether remaining thrust exceeds weight” but must also consider attitude controllability and remaining range — because a diversion needs extra energy.

Torque imbalance is more dangerous than insufficient thrust

The symmetry of the eight-arm layout means that any single-point failure creates a moment imbalance about the centre of the fuselage. The control system must restore balance by reducing thrust on the opposite side, which further reduces total thrust. This process is coupled — the smaller the thrust margin, the fewer the available compensation means and the worse the attitude-holding capability.

This explains an apparently contradictory phenomenon: the fault tolerance of a multirotor is not shown by “being able to fly” but by “being able to land smoothly”. After losing one arm, the aircraft may still hover, but it no longer has the capability to continue the mission and must enter the nearest-diversion procedure. The design goal of the safety architecture is precisely to ensure that this “dignified exit” path always exists.

Of the five residual risks, the first three are at the design level and the last two at the operational level. This forms the transition point from “designed safety” to “operational safety”, and is what the operational safety argument in Chapter 08 must take up.

06Chapter Summary

Three flight-control channels, 12 battery groups and three data links form layered redundancy. Voting can tolerate one wrong channel, on the premise that at most one is wrong — when two are wrong in the same direction, 2oo3 will decide wrongly, the most fragile cell of the whole architecture.

The thrust margin is worked out in concrete numbers: single-rotor and single-arm failures are controllable, and the margin turns negative after the loss of two arms. Fault tolerance is not shown by “still being able to fly” but by “being able to land smoothly”.
