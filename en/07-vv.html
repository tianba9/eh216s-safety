Verification Proves Requirements, Not Flight Hours

Every step of the safety analysis produces requirements: triple redundancy must tolerate one erroneous channel, the battery must block thermal propagation, and a diversion must be completable within the remaining energy. Verification is the act of proving these requirements one by one. Flight hours cannot substitute for this.

01The V-Model & Verification Chain

The left side is design, the right side verification, and the two speak to each other at the same level. If any level lacks a corresponding verification activity, the requirements at that level are unproved.

02Choosing Means of Compliance

Airworthiness certification does not use the blanket phrase “passed the test”; it divides means of proof into several means of compliance. Which one is chosen depends on the nature of the requirement.

Table 7-1　Common means of compliance and their applicable situations
MethodDescriptionExample applicable requirement
Design reviewExamine drawings, architecture and interface documentsWhether the three flight-control sets are physically isolated and wiring separated
Analysis / calculationUse models to compute performance and loadsStructural strength, thrust margin, energy balance
Laboratory testComponent-level environmental and functional testsBattery thermal-runaway trigger, motor endurance, EMC
Ground testFunctional and structural tests of the whole aircraft on the groundStatic loading, landing-gear drop test, crash test
Flight testVerification under real flight conditionsPerformance envelope, hover stability, failure response
Simulator testHuman-in-the-loop or hardware-in-the-loop simulationRemote-crew takeover, extreme-scenario rehearsal
InspectionVisual or gauge confirmationLabels, clearances, assembly correctness
Equipment qualificationRely on existing qualification evidenceExisting certification of standard parts and finished equipment

A common mistake is to treat the flight test as a universal means. In fact, catastrophic failure scenarios cannot be verified by actually flying them — no one would cut an arm in flight to prove that “single-arm failure remains controllable”, unless there were ample safety redundancy and an uninhabited area. Such requirements are met mainly by analysis + ground rig + hardware-in-the-loop.

04Fault Injection & Scenario Testing

For a highly redundant system, the most valuable verification means is not “flying normally” but “deliberately making it fail”. The value of fault injection is that it turns redundancy on paper into observable behaviour.

Table 7-3　Fault-injection items to be covered
Injection itemMethodObservation focus
Single motor failureCut one ESC output on the rigWhether control reconfiguration is smooth and attitude jumps occur
Dual motor failure (same arm / opposite side)Combined injectionDifferences between the two cases, verifying the fault-tolerance boundary
Single link lossShield one data linkWhether switching is seamless and alerts correct
All three links lostShield all linksTriggering and execution of the fail-safe programme
Sensor biasInject a fixed offset / driftWhether cross-checking identifies and rejects it
GNSS spoofingInject false positioning signalsWhether the fusion algorithm rejects them and triggers degradation
Flight-control channel disagreementForce one channel to output a wrong commandWhether voting isolates that channel and whether misjudgement occurs

Scenario testing should follow a convergent path of “logical scenario → concrete scenario → boundary scenario”: first define the parameter space (wind speed, payload, temperature, route curvature), then sample it into executable test cases, and finally densify sampling near the boundaries. The difficulty has never been passing typical scenarios, but proving that the boundaries have been covered.

05Statistical Weight of Operating Evidence

As of June 2026, more than 90,000 cumulative sorties with zero accidents Company claim. This is a respectable figure, but what it can and cannot support needs to be worked out.

Table 7-4　Estimate of the statistical power of the operating data
MetricValueNote
Cumulative sorties90 000Company basis, including test flights and training
Flight time per sortie25 minBased on a full-load sightseeing mission
Cumulative flight hours≈ 37 500 h90 000 × 25 / 60
Catastrophic-failure target10-9/fhTypical order for transport-category aircraft
Hours needed for statistical proof≈ 109 hSample required to demonstrate that order with zero failures
GapAbout four orders of magnitudeOperating data are far from sufficient alone to prove the target is met
The correct interpretation

The significance of 90,000 sorties is not to prove 10-9 but lies in three things: first, showing there is no obvious systemic defect (otherwise it should have surfaced); second, providing input to reliability-growth models and supporting engineering estimates of failure rates; third, supporting iteration of operating procedures and crew training. It belongs to the evidence of “operational safety argument”, not to the evidence of “quantitative proof of failure probability”. Confusing the two is the most common logical leap in safety argumentation.

06Blind Spots in Verification

Every verification plan has gaps in coverage. Explicitly identifying blind spots is more credible than claiming “fully verified”.

Table 7-5　Three classes of verification blind spot that are hard to cover
Blind spotWhy it is hard to verifyFeasible alternative
Multiple concurrent failuresCombinatorial explosion makes exhaustive enumeration impossible; actual flight is impracticableRisk-based sampled combination injection + simulation
Long-term degradationBattery fade and structural fatigue take years of operation to appearAccelerated life testing + operational monitoring + periodic inspection
Extreme weatherFlight verification is dangerous and hard to reproduceWeather-limit restrictions + wind tunnel/simulation + conservative envelope

The common principle for handling these three blind spots is “substituting operating limits for verification”: since safety under extreme conditions cannot be proved, those conditions are excluded from the ODD. This is precisely the starting point of the Chapter 08 operational safety argument.

07Chapter Summary

Verification proves requirements one by one, not cumulative flight hours. Every level on the right of the V-model must have matching evidence; a missing level means that level's requirements are unproved; catastrophic scenarios cannot be verified by actual flight.

Ninety thousand sorties convert to roughly 37,500 flight hours, four orders of magnitude below the statistical requirement for 10-9. It is operational evidence supporting reliability growth and cannot replace the quantitative proof of failure probability.
