Taking “Zero Accidents” Apart

A pilotless passenger-carrying aircraft removes “human error” — the greatest source of uncertainty — from the vehicle, and with it the last manual backstop. Safety responsibility is therefore transferred entirely to automation, redundant architecture and the ground remote crew. Using the eight-step method of systems safety engineering, this report examines whether that allocation of responsibility is argued convincingly enough.

01Five Safety Conclusions

Conclusion 01 · Design premise

Removing the pilot is the first-principles choice for the whole safety architecture

There are no flight controls on board; the aircraft flies the entire route automatically along a preset path, while the ground remote crew only monitors and takes over in an emergency Standard. This choice eliminates human failures such as fatigue, misoperation and spatial disorientation, at the cost of removing the last “human-in-the-loop” backstop. Every safety design must compensate for this.

Conclusion 02 · Architectural weak point

Triple redundancy addresses random failures but not common-cause failures

Three independent flight-control channels with a voting mechanism can reduce the probability of a single random failure to a very low level Official. But if the three systems share the same software, the same power supply or the same installation area, 1oo3 degrades to 1oo1. Common Cause Analysis (CCA) is the real test of this architecture, and no conclusion on it appears in the public material.

Conclusion 03 · Nature of evidence

90,000 sorties are operating evidence, not a safety proof

At the catastrophic-failure target of 10-9 per flight hour, statistically “proving” compliance would require data on the order of 109 hours; 90,000 sorties at 25 minutes each amount to roughly 37,500 flight hours Author's estimate. This supports a reliability-growth argument but cannot replace a quantitative analysis of failure probability.

Conclusion 04 · Hazard focus

The three most severe hazards: loss of control in flight, energy failure, and post-crash occupant protection

The first two are controlled by architecture (redundancy, isolation, degradation), and the third by crashworthiness design. The special condition does add structural-protection requirements for the unmanned passenger cabin Standard, but public information contains the fewest quantitative figures on energy-absorbing structures, restraint systems and whole-aircraft recovery measures, making this the weakest link in the safety argument.

Conclusion 05 · Risk attribution

Residual risk lies mainly at the operational boundary, not in the hardware

Weather, segregated airspace, crowd separation at the vertiport and remote-crew human factors cannot be solved by adding redundancy; they can only be controlled through ODD definition and operating procedures. They determine whether “designed safety” can be converted into “operational safety”.

02Scope & Boundaries

The most common mistake in safety analysis is to begin by “adding redundancy”. This report first sets three boundaries and only then proceeds with the analysis — boundaries determine the scope within which every conclusion holds.

Table 0-1　Three boundaries: object, method and evidence
BoundaryScope definedConsequence of crossing
Object boundarySystem level: the aircraft itself + ground control station + data link. Excludes the operating organisation, maintenance system and low-altitude infrastructureMisjudging a system problem as a design defect, or the reverse
Method boundaryFHA assigns severity, STPA finds systemic gaps, FTA derives cut sets, FMEA covers components, CCA checks common causes; the five have distinct roles and do not replace one anotherFTA alone misses the case where “all parts are healthy but the control logic is wrong”
Evidence boundaryReconstructed solely from public material, an independent third-party recomputation; the manufacturer's FHA/PSSA/SSA/FMEA and compliance-verification documents are not availableCiting a recomputation result as an authority conclusion

Three self-imposed constraints

  • No probabilistic quantification. Without failure data and architecture details, the fault tree yields only cut sets and a qualitative ranking, not a probability for the top event.
  • No inference about undisclosed items. Whether the aircraft has a whole-aircraft parachute, on-board fire suppression, the power-supply topology or heterogeneous software — anything not stated is marked “undisclosed”, never inferred to mean “absent”.
  • Operating data are not treated as proof. 90,000 sorties support reliability growth, not a 10-9 statistical demonstration.

04Coverage Across Five Safety Domains
