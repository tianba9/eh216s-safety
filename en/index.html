Taking “Zero Accidents” Apart

Removing the pilot removes one of the largest sources of human error, and with it the last human backstop. Safety responsibility therefore shifts to automation, redundant architecture and the ground crew. This report applies the eight-step systems-safety method to test whether that redistribution is argued convincingly enough.

01Five Safety Conclusions

01 · First principle

Removing the pilot is the first architectural decision

It eliminates fatigue, misoperation and spatial disorientation, and removes the last in-loop backstop. Every later safety measure compensates for that choice.

02 · Architectural weak point

Redundancy handles random faults, not common causes

Three flight-control channels with 2oo3 voting tolerate one wrong output. If all three share a software defect, power supply or installation zone, 1oo3 degrades to 1oo1. Common-cause analysis is the real test.

03 · Nature of evidence

90,000 sorties are operating evidence, not a statistical proof

They correspond to roughly 37,500 flight hours. Demonstrating a 10⁻⁹ catastrophic-failure target statistically would need nine orders more hours. The record supports reliability growth, not probability quantification.

04 · Hazard focus

Loss of control, energy failure and post-crash protection dominate

The first two are handled by architecture; the third by crashworthiness. Public quantitative data on the third is the thinnest part of the case.

05 · Residual risk

Most residual risk sits at the operational boundary

Weather, segregated airspace, crowd separation and remote-crew human factors cannot be fixed by adding hardware. They are controlled through the ODD and operating procedures.

02Scope & Boundaries

Safety analysis usually goes wrong when it starts by adding redundancy. This report first fixes three boundaries, because boundaries determine the validity of every conclusion.

Table 0-1　Three boundaries
BoundaryDefinitionConsequence of crossing
ObjectSystem level: aircraft, ground control station and data link; not the operator or infrastructureSystem problems misread as design defects, or the reverse
MethodFHA grades, STPA finds systemic gaps, FTA derives cut sets, FMEA covers parts, CCA checks common causesFTA alone misses “all parts healthy, control logic wrong”
EvidencePublic materials only; an independent recomputation, not the manufacturer's safety caseTreating a recomputation as an authority conclusion

Three constraints

  • No probabilistic quantification. Failure-rate data and architecture details are missing, so the fault tree yields cut sets and qualitative ranking only.
  • No inference from silence. Where a feature is not disclosed, it is marked “undisclosed”, never “absent”.
  • Operating data are not proof. 90,000 sorties support reliability growth, not a 10⁻⁹ demonstration.
