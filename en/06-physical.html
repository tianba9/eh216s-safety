The Two Worst Outcomes: Fire and Impact

The catastrophic consequences of a passenger-carrying aircraft ultimately take just two physical forms — fire and impact. This chapter analyses both paths in depth: how thermal runaway spreads from a single cell to the whole aircraft, and how much energy the structure must absorb in a crash. Both are given numerically derivable figures.

01Focus A: Battery Thermal Runaway

The special condition explicitly sets out thermal-runaway protection and redundant power-supply requirements for the battery system under the “powerplant” category Standard. This is not a generic statement — it takes “the battery will burn” as a given premise and requires a design that is “not fatal even when it burns”.

02Runaway Chain & Break Points

Thermal runaway is not an instantaneous event but a reaction chain with a definite sequence. The opportunity for safety design lies in every breakable link along the chain.

Three questions most in need of verification

① Is a physical thermal barrier installed between groups, or is spacing alone relied upon? ② Is there a directed venting channel that ejects matter outside the aircraft rather than into the cabin? ③ How many seconds of warning does the BMS provide — a figure that directly determines whether the pilot (here, the remote crew and the autonomous system) still has a window to respond. No public data are available for any of the three.

03Focus B: Crash Safety

The special condition specifically adds structural-protection (crashworthiness) requirements for the unmanned passenger cabin under the “structure, design and construction” category Standard. This requirement does not exist in conventional UAV certification, because a UAV has no occupants to protect.

The core tension that crashworthiness design must resolve is: at the same impact speed, the deceleration borne by the occupant depends on the stroke over which the structure deforms. The longer the stroke, the lower the deceleration and the lighter the injury. But a multirotor has no wings and no fuselage longerons, so the space available for deformation is extremely limited — this is the third penalty imposed by the configuration.

04Energy Absorption, Quantified

Kinetic energy is calculated for a vertical impact at a 620 kg all-up mass, and the mean deceleration for a given deformation stroke is derived in reverse. The short-term human tolerance limit is about 20 g (in the spinal direction), from which the minimum required energy-absorbing stroke can be judged.

Table 6-2　Kinetic energy and deceleration in a vertical crash (author's estimate)
Impact speedKinetic energyStroke 0.1 mStroke 0.3 mAssessment

12 m/s44.6 kJ73.4 g24.5 gExceeds the limit for both strokes

Formulae: E = ½mv², a = v²/(2s). g = 9.81 m/s². The assessment baseline is the short-term tolerance of about 20 g in the spinal direction; the actual limit varies with exposure time and restraint type.

What this table shows

To keep an 8 m/s vertical impact within human tolerance, the energy-absorbing stroke must approach 0.3 m. On a multirotor only 1.93 m tall with a compact cabin, a 0.3 m crush zone means the lower fuselage must be deliberately designed as a crushable structure and the seats decoupled from the airframe. This cannot be solved by adding a block of foam; it is a design decision at the overall-layout level.

More notable is the 12 m/s row: once impact speed exceeds a certain threshold, no stroke can keep deceleration within the safe range. The only remaining protection is to avoid hitting the ground at that speed — through fail-safe design, diversion-area selection and envelope limits. This is the quantitative proof that “passive safety cannot stand alone”.

An obvious omission

General aviation and some eVTOL types fit a whole-aircraft parachute as a last resort “when attitude cannot be controlled”. No public material indicates that the EH216-S has a whole-aircraft parachute, nor any ejection-seat-type solution. This cannot be read directly as “absent”, but at least shows that the means has not been disclosed as a selling point. If there is indeed no whole-aircraft parachute, then crash safety rests entirely on the threefold combination of “controlled diversion + energy-absorbing structure + restraint system”, placing correspondingly higher reliability demands on the fail-safe.

05High-Voltage Electrical & EMC

The all-electric architecture introduces two risks not native to conventional aircraft: high-voltage electric shock and electromagnetic interference.

The special condition requires the data link to meet anti-interference standards and a triple-redundant communications architecture Standard, effectively elevating EMC from a “design recommendation” to a “certification requirement”. For an aircraft whose control commands are entirely carried over the link, interference with the link is equivalent to loss of control.

06Fire Protection & Materials

Fire-protection requirements for a passenger cabin differ fundamentally from those for a UAV: the latter need only protect equipment, while the former must protect the occupants' escape or survival until landing in a fire.

  • Material flame retardance: cabin interior, seats and harness sleeving must meet flame-retardance and low-smoke-toxicity requirements. Multirotors make extensive use of carbon-fibre composites, whose combustion behaviour differs from metal structures and requires dedicated assessment.
  • Smoke toxicity and visibility: the cabin is cramped, so even a small amount of smoke can seriously impair occupants' judgement and breathing. Smoke-extraction path design is more critical than extinguishing capability.
  • Extinguishing means: no public information shows an on-board extinguishing system. If indeed absent, fire response relies entirely on “isolation + landing as soon as possible”, making early interruption of thermal runaway the only effective line of defence.
  • Emergency evacuation: the gull-wing door design facilitates rapid egress, but whether it can be mechanically opened from inside after a power loss, and whether occupants can operate it themselves, are key items requiring verification — especially given the premise that occupants are entirely passive.
An inference from one design logic

The combination of “no pilot + no extinguishing system + a cramped cabin” means this aircraft's fire-safety strategy can only be “never occurs, never exceeds one group”, rather than “extinguish after it occurs”. This explains why the special condition writes thermal-runaway protection directly into the powerplant requirements — it is the only viable line of defence.

07Chapter Summary

Catastrophic consequences ultimately take just two physical forms: fire and impact. The thermal-runaway chain has four break points; the design goal is not “non-occurrence” but confining the failure to one group and one module, buying time for a diversion.

The quantitative conclusion on crash is hard: 8 m/s requires about 0.3 m of energy-absorbing stroke, while at 12 m/s any stroke exceeds the limit — passive safety cannot stand alone. With no pilot, no extinguishing system and a cramped cabin combined, the fire strategy can only be “no occurrence, no propagation”.
