Twelve Hazards, One Control Structure, One Fault Tree

The output of hazard analysis is not a long table but three judgements: which hazards are unacceptable, which control links may issue wrong commands, and which failure paths bypass redundancy. This chapter presents these three in turn.

01FHA: Twelve Hazards

Severity is divided into five levels following transport-category practice: catastrophic, hazardous, major, minor and no safety effect. Probability targets decrease with severity; catastrophic items take 10-9 per flight hour.

Table 3-1　Functional Hazard Assessment (FHA)
No.HazardPhaseSeverityTarget probabilityControl approach
H01Loss of attitude control in flightCruiseCatastrophic10-9Triple-redundant flight control + voting + envelope protection
H02Total thrust lossAll phasesCatastrophic10-916-rotor distributed propulsion + 12 isolated battery groups
H03Fire from battery thermal runawayAll phasesCatastrophic10-9Thermal-runaway warning, isolation between groups, venting and flame retardance
H04Collision with an obstacle or another aircraftCruiseCatastrophic10-9Segregated airspace + filed routes + detect and avoid
H05Fatal injury to occupants in a crashLandingCatastrophic10-9Crashworthy structure + restraints + energy absorption
H06Partial thrust loss leading to uncontrollable yawAll phasesHazardous10-7Control reconfiguration + degraded flight + diversion to the nearest site
H07Data-link lossCruiseHazardous10-7Triple link + autonomous fail-safe on link loss
H08Navigation interference or spoofing causing deviationCruiseHazardous10-7Multi-source navigation fusion + consistency checks
H09Strong wind disturbance beyond control capabilityTake-off / cruiseHazardous10-7Weather limits + wind estimation + envelope limits
H10Ground personnel entering the rotor area during take-off and landingTake-off / landingHazardous10-7Site isolation + ground guidance + pre-take-off confirmation
H11Unplanned landing causing injury to third parties on the groundAll phasesMajor10-5Diversion-area selection + routing to avoid densely populated areas
H12Occupants not restrained or accidentally opening the doorAll phasesMajor10-5Restraint monitoring + door interlocks + pre-take-off checks

Of the five catastrophic hazards, H01 and H02 are controlled by architecture, H03 by the battery system and thermal management, H04 by airspace and operations, and H05 by structural crashworthiness. The four classes of control belong to entirely different professional disciplines — which explains why whole-aircraft safety cannot be accomplished by a single team.

02STPA Control Structure

The first step in STPA is to describe the system as a hierarchical control structure: who commands whom and who feeds back status to whom. The particular feature of the EH216-S is that the control chain crosses two physical spaces — “on board” and “on the ground”.

03Unsafe Control Actions

The core of STPA is to ask, for each control action, under what circumstances “providing it”, “not providing it”, “providing it too early”, “too late” or “for too long” leads to a hazard. The table below lists five typical cases.

Table 3-2　Typical Unsafe Control Actions (UCA) and causal scenarios
No.Control actionUnsafe typeCausal scenarioConsequence
U1Providing thrust commandDuration too longContinuous correction under wind disturbance, no degradation even after actuator saturationMotor overload, attitude divergence
U2Terminating flight / triggering diversionNot providedFail-Safe criteria too broad, health state misjudged as normalContinuing to fly with a fault
U3Switching flight-control channelImproperly providedVoting logic selects the wrong channel under dual-channel failureFaulty channel connected, loss of attitude control
U4Issuing a route change remotelyProvided too lateLink delay combined with inadequate crew situation awarenessEntering a no-fly zone or obstacle area
U5Take-off clearanceProvided too earlyGround checks incomplete, occupants not restrainedDoor opening, occupant injury

U2 and U3 are the two most concerning because both occur while the “system believes itself normal”. Traditional FMEA cannot find such problems — all components pass self-checks. This is precisely why STPA exists: it analyses defects in the control logic, not damage to components.

04Fault Tree: Uncommanded Ground Impact

Taking “uncommanded ground impact of the aircraft” as the top event, three intermediate events are developed below it. Under each intermediate event the dominant cut sets are listed as a check list for architecture design.

The value of the fault tree lies not in the diagram itself but in the three questions it forces: are the three flight-control channels truly independent? Can thermal propagation really be blocked between the 12 battery groups? Can an arm crack be detected before the next flight? These three questions point to Chapters 04, 06 and 07 respectively.

05Common-Cause Analysis

The enemy of redundancy is not random failure but common cause. If the three systems share any single element, 1oo3 degrades to 1oo1 at that moment.

Table 3-3　Four common-cause channels and isolation measures
Common-cause channelSpecific manifestationIsolation measureVerifiability
Shared software originThree flight-control channels run the same code; the same defect is triggered simultaneouslyHeterogeneous development, independent teams, independent tool chains, diverse algorithmsVerifiable through development-process review
Shared power supplyThree systems fed from one bus; one short circuit removes all powerIndependent power sources and distribution channels, physically separated wiringVerifiable through architecture diagrams and fault injection
Physical environmentThree sensors in one bay, simultaneously heated, vibrated and exposed to moistureSeparate-bay installation, vibration isolation, higher environmental-protection ratingVerifiable through environmental testing
Maintenance errorThe same incorrect procedure applied to three identical componentsError-proofing, independent inspection, differentiated proceduresVerifiable through maintenance-programme review
The most critical gap

Public material states “three independent flight-control systems” and “full backup of key components” Official, but does not disclose whether the three systems use heterogeneous software. If the software is of common origin, “independence” solves only random hardware failures, not software common causes — and software defects are precisely the risk hardest to detect statistically in a Level A system. This should be the first-priority question for the manufacturer or the authority.

06Chapter Summary

Of the 12 hazards, 5 are catastrophic. They are controlled respectively by architecture, by the battery and thermal management, and by crashworthy structure — these four classes of means belong to different professions, so whole-aircraft safety cannot be accomplished by a single team.

STPA draws the control chain as a structure spanning on-board and ground; of the five classes of unsafe control action, the most dangerous is a wrong decision made while the “system believes itself normal”. Of the nine cut sets in the fault tree, three are single points that pierce redundancy.
